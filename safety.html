Checkpoint 1 of 5 · ~8 min

Passwords & Account Security

  • Use long passphrases (12+ characters) instead of simple words.
  • Never reuse the same password across different accounts.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) wherever it's offered.
  • Never share your password — not even with a friend, "just this once."
  • Consider a password manager instead of notes apps or paper.
▶ YouTube

Watch: Strong Passwords (~2:30) — a quick primer on why passwords matter and how to build a good one.

Quick Check

4 questions

1. Which of these passwords is strongest?

Long, unpredictable passphrases mixing words, numbers, and symbols are far harder to crack than common words or patterns.

2. True or False: It's fine to reuse one strong password across your email, school portal, and social media.

If one site is breached, a reused password lets attackers into all your other accounts too.

3. What does turning on two-factor authentication (2FA) do?

Even if someone steals your password, 2FA means they still can't get in without the second code — usually sent to your phone.

4. A classmate asks to borrow your login just this once to check a grade. Best move?

Anyone who has your password can act as you — check messages, change settings, or get you blamed for something you didn't do.
Checkpoint 2 of 5 · ~8 min

Phishing & Scams

  • Phishing = fake messages designed to trick you into clicking a link or giving up information.
  • Red flags: urgent language, unexpected attachments, mismatched sender addresses, "you won a prize!"
  • Check where a link actually goes before clicking it.
  • Never enter your password after clicking a link from an email or text.
  • Report suspicious messages to a teacher or IT instead of replying.
▶ YouTube

Watch: Teach students to recognize phishing and scams (~2:30) — spotting the warning signs before you click.

Quick Check

4 questions

1. An email says: "URGENT: your account will be deleted in 1 hour — click here now!" This is a classic sign of:

Manufactured urgency is one of the biggest phishing tells — it's designed to make you click before you think.

2. True or False: If a message looks like it's from a friend's account, it's always safe to click their links.

Friends' accounts get hacked too. A strange link from a real contact can still be dangerous.

3. What's the safest first step when you get a suspicious link?

Verifying outside the message (a call, a different app) or reporting to a teacher/IT keeps you safe without giving attackers anything.

4. Which detail is a red flag in a "You won a prize!" email?

Real prizes and services never need your password. That request is the giveaway.
Checkpoint 3 of 5 · ~8 min

Privacy & Sharing Online

  • Think before you post — screenshots and shares can last long after you delete the original.
  • Don't share your address, full schedule, or real-time location publicly.
  • Check privacy settings on your apps regularly; defaults change.
  • "Friends only" can still reach more people than you expect.
  • Never post to embarrass or bully someone — it's permanent and it's serious.
▶ YouTube

Watch: Private and Personal Information (~3:00) (Common Sense Education) — what's safe to share online and what isn't.

Quick Check

4 questions

1. Which is safest to include on a public profile?

The less identifying detail that's public, the harder it is for a stranger to find or track you.

2. True or False: Once you delete a post, it's guaranteed gone everywhere.

Screenshots, shares, and cached copies can outlive the original post by years.

3. Best move before posting a photo or story about someone else?

Consent matters online just like in person — a quick check-in avoids embarrassing or hurting someone.

4. A stranger online asks what school you go to. You should:

Strangers don't need this information, and a trusted adult can help you handle it if it feels off.
Checkpoint 4 of 5 · ~8 min

Device & Wi-Fi Safety

  • Lock your device with a PIN, passcode, or biometric.
  • Keep apps and your OS updated — updates patch security holes.
  • Avoid logging into bank or school accounts on public Wi-Fi.
  • Only download apps from official app stores.
  • Always log out of shared or public computers when you're done.
▶ YouTube

Watch: Public Wi-Fi Networks - Security Tips (~1:30) (Federal Trade Commission) — staying safe on networks you don't control.

Quick Check

4 questions

1. Why update your apps and operating system regularly?

Most updates exist specifically to fix security weaknesses that have already been discovered.

2. True or False: Public Wi-Fi at a coffee shop is just as secure as your home network.

Open public networks make it easier for others on the same network to intercept your traffic.

3. Before walking away from a shared library computer, you should:

Closing a tab doesn't log you out — the next person could open your email or accounts directly.

4. What's the safest place to download a new app?

Official stores screen apps for malware; outside links and attachments are common ways devices get infected.
Checkpoint 5 of 5 · ~10 min

IT Ergonomics

  • Top of your screen at or just below eye level, about arm's length away.
  • Feet flat on the floor, knees around 90°, back supported.
  • Elbows close to your body, wrists straight while typing — not bent up or down.
  • Follow the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
  • Get up and move every 30–60 minutes, even briefly.
▶ YouTube

Watch: Computer Ergonomics as Fast As Possible (~5:00) — quick fixes for a healthier desk setup.

Quick Check

4 questions

1. About how far should your screen be from your eyes?

Roughly an arm's length reduces eye strain while keeping text easy to read.

2. True or False: Wrists should be bent upward while typing for better speed.

Straight, neutral wrists reduce strain — bending them up or down over time can lead to pain and injury.

3. What's the 20-20-20 rule for?

Every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds — it gives your eyes a break from close-up focus.

4. What's the ideal knee angle while seated at a desk?

A roughly 90-degree knee angle with feet flat on the floor supports healthy circulation and posture.
